Stainless Steel Grade for Industrial Kitchen Equipment: AISI 304 or 316?

September 21, 2026

"Stainless steel" is not one material — it's a family of alloys with very different chemical compositions and corrosion resistance. If you buy kitchen equipment based on the label "stainless" alone, you may end up with staining, pitting or even rust within a few years. The real question is which grade a given piece of equipment actually needs.

What Is AISI 304 and Where Does It Belong?

AISI 304 is an austenitic stainless steel containing roughly 18% chromium and 8% nickel, and it's the workhorse of the industrial kitchen industry. It handles normal kitchen humidity, detergents and food acids well, making it the right choice — both in performance and cost — for worktables, shelving, cabinets, dishwashing sinks and the body of most cooking equipment.

What 316 Adds: Why Molybdenum Matters

The key difference in AISI 316 is roughly 2-3% molybdenum added to the alloy. This addition significantly improves resistance to pitting corrosion caused by chloride ions — in plain terms, salt. That's why 316 is the preferred grade for salted food processing lines, brine and pickle production, coastal facilities, and equipment shipped to humid, salty export markets such as Gulf countries or seaside cities.

Which Grade for Which Equipment?

The general rule: if the surface won't see chloride exposure, 304 is more than enough and avoids unnecessary cost. But for dishwasher interiors that constantly sit in hot detergent water, fish and seafood processing tables, brine tanks, and outdoor equipment at coastal restaurants, choosing 316 up front prevents the staining and pitting that would otherwise show up within a few years.

Cost and Verifying the Material Certificate

316 typically costs 15-25% more than 304 due to raw material prices, so it pays to reserve that premium for surfaces that genuinely need it rather than the whole installation. More importantly, the market has low-grade magnetic steels — such as 201 or 430 series — sold under the vague label "stainless." Always request a mill certificate from your supplier before ordering; it's the only way to confirm you're paying for the grade you actually need, not a weaker substitute.
